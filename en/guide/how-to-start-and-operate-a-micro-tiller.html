How to Start and Operate a Micro Tiller: A First-Timer's Guide

Published: 2026-11-15Our Products: Micro Tiller

The first ten minutes decide how the day goes

Most people who struggle with a new micro tiller struggle with the same things: they start it in the wrong order, they set the depth wrong, and they work the soil too fast. None of that is difficult to fix, and most of it can be learned in one afternoon. This guide walks through starting, setting up and operating a machine from our range.

Everything here applies to the whole range. The 186cc Tiger 1WG-6.3B-T adds electric starting to the manual recoil starter; everything else in the range starts by hand.

Starting the engine, in the right order

1. Put the machine on level ground, in gear, and clear of people. The tines are rotating tools and they do not stop when the engine stops. 2. Check the fuel level and the oil level. Open the tap and check that the fuel filter bowl is clean. 3. Set the controls to neutral, throttle closed, and the depth lever to its shallowest position. 4. Engage the clutch lever — on these machines the clutch is normally open, so it is engaged when the lever is pushed away from the handle. 5. Open the fuel tap, and if the engine is cold, close the choke. Cold start needs a richer mixture; that is what the choke is for. 6. Pull the recoil starter. Pull firmly and smoothly rather than yanking it — a hard yank is what breaks starter cords. The 186cc Tiger 1WG-6.3B-T can be started electrically instead. 7. Open the choke as soon as the engine catches, then let it run for a minute before working the throttle.

If the engine will not start, work through it in this order: is there fuel in the tank, is the fuel tap open, is the air filter clean, is the choke being used correctly, is the spark plug clean. Those five cover nearly every case, and they are in the order that is quickest to check.

Setting the depth and the gear

Working depth is set by the depth lever on the handle, and it should be adjusted with the engine stopped. Start shallow on the first pass and deepen it as the soil proves easy to work — a machine that is set too deep on the first pass will fight the operator and can bury itself.

The gearbox on every machine in the range has two forward gears and one reverse, with a friction-plate clutch that is normally open. The low gear is for breaking new ground and for heavy soil; the high gear is for the finish pass and for transport across the plot. The reverse is for getting out of a corner or a rutted patch without turning the machine around.

The 170cc petrol 1WG-4.0AQ works to 100-250 mm, the 178cc diesel 1WG-4.1A to 150-300 mm, and the 186cc diesel 1WG-6.3A to 180-350 mm.

Working technique that gets more done per hour

  • Walk at the speed of the machine, not your own. The tines are designed to be driven by the engine; if you pull the handle back, the blades leave the ground and the work gets worse.
  • Overlap each pass slightly. A narrow overlap is what produces a flat, even bed instead of a series of ridges.
  • Work in the order the ground needs it, not the order that is convenient. Break the rough ground first with the low gear and a shallow setting, then finish it on the high gear at the depth the crop needs.
  • Do not work soil that is too wet. It smears instead of cutting, and it packs down again as it dries. This is the single most common cause of a disappointing first season.
  • Keep the machine off the headland if the headland is where you turn. Turning on the headland with the tines in the ground is how a blade gets bent.

Working around obstacles and in the field

If the machine hits a stone or a root, the correct response is to release the clutch and lift the handle slightly rather than to force it. Forcing the machine digs in and stalls the engine; lifting the tines and easing forward is faster.

For narrow work between rows or in an orchard, the narrow-frame 170cc petrol 1WG-4.0AQ at 600-900 mm (adjustable) and 68 kg is much easier to control than the larger machines, and it can be lifted and repositioned by one person.

Stopping safely

To stop, take the throttle down, disengage the clutch, and let the engine idle for a moment before switching it off. Never stop the engine with the tines buried in the soil — the rebound can damage the drive. On the 186cc Tiger 1WG-6.3B-T, use the electric starter to shut down rather than pulling the cord.

Common problems and what they actually mean

Engine will not start. Fuel in the tank, tap open, air filter clean, correct choke, clean spark plug — check those five in that order.

Engine runs but stalls under load. Usually a dirty air filter or a loose fuel connection. On a diesel, also check the fuel filter for water.

Engine smokes. Blue smoke points to oil in the fuel — check the oil level and the oil cap seal. White smoke on a cold start is condensation and clears once the engine is warm.

Machine runs but the blades do not turn. Disengage the clutch lever and check that the drive belt or chain has not come off.

Vibration. Almost always a bent tine or a worn blade group. Check the blades before you look anywhere else.

Tines will not go deep enough. The soil may be too wet, the depth lever may not be fully released, or the depth stop may have been set for a different position when the machine was last used.

One last safety note

Never reach into a running machine. Stop the engine before clearing a blade, adjusting the depth, or checking for a wrapped wire, and never lift or carry the machine while the engine is running.

Frequently asked questions

Answers to the questions buyers ask us most.

What order do I start a micro tiller in?

Level ground, in gear and clear of people; fuel and oil checked and the fuel tap open; controls in neutral with the throttle closed and the depth lever at its shallowest; clutch engaged; choke closed if the engine is cold; then pull the recoil starter firmly and smoothly. Open the choke as soon as the engine catches and let it run for a minute.

Should the choke be closed when starting a cold engine?

Yes. A cold start needs a richer mixture, and that is what the choke is for. Open it as soon as the engine catches.

Why does the engine stall when the blades meet resistance?

Check the fuel and air side first, in this order: fuel in the tank, fuel tap open, air filter clean, choke used correctly, spark plug clean. On a diesel, also check the fuel filter for water.

How do I set the working depth?

With the depth lever on the handle, and only with the engine stopped. Start shallow on the first pass and deepen it as the soil proves easy to work. A machine set too deep on the first pass fights the operator and can bury itself.

Which gear should I use for new ground?

The low gear for breaking new ground and heavy soil, the high gear for the finish pass and for moving across the plot. The reverse gets you out of a corner or a rutted patch without turning the machine around.

What should I do if the machine hits a stone or a root?

Release the clutch and lift the handle slightly rather than forcing it. Forcing the machine digs in and stalls the engine; lifting the tines and easing forward is faster.

Can I work the soil when it is wet?

No. Working soil that is too wet smears it instead of cutting it, and it packs down again as it dries. Wait for the soil to break cleanly in your hand before starting.

Is it safe to stop the engine with the tines in the soil?

No. Take the throttle down, disengage the clutch and let the engine idle before switching it off. The rebound from stopping with the tines buried can damage the drive.
