Gasoline vs Diesel Micro Tiller: Which One to Buy

Published: 2026-11-15Our Products: Micro Tiller

The decision that matters before size

Displacement gets all the attention, but the fuel type decides more about your day than the horsepower does. It decides what you can buy at the pump, what you carry down to the field, how the engine behaves when the soil is wet, and what the machine costs to keep running for a season. Only after that does engine size matter.

Our range covers both, in the same frame family: the petrol machines are the 170cc and 173cc models, the diesel machines run from 170cc up to 186cc. The micro tiller category page lists every model with its full specification.

What actually changes when you switch from petrol to diesel

A petrol engine in these machines is a small, light four-stroke or two-stroke unit with a simple carburettor and a magneto. A diesel engine is compression-ignition: the fuel is compressed hard enough to ignite on its own, which removes the spark plug and gives the engine a different character under load.

The practical consequences:

  • Weight. A diesel engine of the same class is heavier. The 170cc petrol 1WG-4.0AQ weighs 68 kg, while the 170cc diesel 1WG-4.0A weighs 95 kg. The difference matters when one person loads the machine into a pickup or carries it over a bund.
  • Starting. Both types are air-cooled single-cylinder engines and both start by hand on most models. Diesel engines are more forgiving of slow cranking in the cold, and the 186cc Tiger 1WG-6.3B-T adds electric starting on top of the recoil starter for anyone working at first light.
  • Behaviour under load. A compression-ignition engine holds its speed when the blades hit a wet strip or a root, because it does not depend on the spark staying strong as the RPM drops. In a hard, wet field that is the difference between finishing the row and stopping to clear the tines.
  • Running cost. A diesel engine generally burns less fuel for the same amount of work, and diesel is often cheaper per litre in agricultural markets. This is the calculation that decides the answer for anyone running a machine every day of the season.

Where petrol wins

Petrol makes sense when the machine is used intermittently, when the work is light, and when petrol is what is actually available. A market with no diesel supply, or a customer who only wants the machine for a few hours a week, is served better by the petrol range. The 170cc petrol 1WG-4.0AQ delivers 4 kW / 5-15 HP @ 3600 rpm and starts by manual recoil, so it needs no battery and no tools in the field. The 173cc petrol 1WG-4.05B is the step up, at 4.05 kW / 5-15 HP @ 3600 rpm and 800-1050 mm (adjustable).

Petrol is also the easier fuel to store and move. A jerrycan of petrol is something almost every farm already has, and a petrol machine can sit for weeks between uses without the fuel system being a concern.

Where diesel wins

Diesel is the right answer for daily work, heavy soil, and long seasons. The 178cc diesel 1WG-4.1A gives 4 kW / 5-15 HP @ 3600 rpm at 800-1050 mm (adjustable) and 150-300 mm, on a frame weighing 115 kg. The 186cc diesel 1WG-6.3A is the open-field machine: 6.3 kW / 5-15 HP @ 3600 rpm, 900-1200 mm (adjustable), and 180-350 mm.

Both diesel sizes keep the same construction as the rest of the range: a ductile-iron gearbox with two forward gears and one reverse, a friction-plate clutch, and 24 dryland rotary blades in 3+1 groups. That gearbox is the part that carries the daily work, and it is the reason a heavier machine is not a bad trade in a field that is worked every day.

A simple way to decide

Ask three questions in order.

1. Can I buy diesel where I am? If the answer is no or uncertain, the question is settled — take the petrol machine. 2. How many hours a day? Under a few hours a week, petrol. Every day through the season, diesel. 3. What am I carrying? If the machine has to be loaded into a vehicle by one or two people and carried over uneven ground, the 27 kg difference between the 170cc petrol and diesel models decides it.

If you are still undecided, tell us your crop, your plot size and what fuel is available in your market, and we will tell you which model we would put on that ground ourselves. Start from the home page or go straight to the full tiller range.

Every machine in the range carries a 1 year warranty, and the construction is common across petrol and diesel versions, so wear parts and consumables are shared.

Frequently asked questions

Answers to the questions buyers ask us most.

Is a diesel micro tiller always more economical?

A diesel engine generally burns less fuel for the same amount of work and diesel is often cheaper per litre in agricultural markets, so running cost usually favours diesel. The petrol machines still win on purchase price and on weight.

Can I convert a petrol micro tiller to diesel?

No. The engine mounting, fuel tank, fuel lines and drive are all dimensioned for one fuel type, and a conversion is not something we support.

Which is lighter, the petrol or the diesel tiller?

The petrol models. The 170cc petrol 1WG-4.0AQ weighs 68 kg, while the 170cc diesel 1WG-4.0A weighs 95 kg. The gap narrows on the larger frames but never reverses.

Does a diesel tiller pull harder in wet soil?

A compression-ignition diesel engine holds its speed when the blades meet resistance, because it does not depend on a spark as the rpm falls. In wet or heavy ground that is the difference between finishing a row and stopping to clear the tines.

Do your diesel tillers have electric starting?

Most start by manual recoil. The 186cc Tiger 1WG-6.3B-T adds electric starting on top of the recoil starter.

Which fuel should a smallholder choose?

Choose the fuel that is available and affordable, then the size that fits the plot. The 170cc petrol 1WG-4.0AQ at 68 kg is the easiest to move by hand; the 178cc diesel 1WG-4.1A is the better daily machine.

Do petrol and diesel tillers share the same gearbox?

Yes. Every tiller in the range uses a ductile-iron gearbox with two forward gears and one reverse and a friction-plate clutch, so wear parts are shared across the fuel types.

Do you build ditching machines and pumps in both fuel types too?

Yes. Our ditching machines and our water pumps cover petrol and diesel variants, because the fuel available differs from one market to the next.
