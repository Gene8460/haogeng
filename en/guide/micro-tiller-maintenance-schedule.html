Micro Tiller Maintenance Schedule: Daily, Weekly and Seasonal

Published: 2026-11-15Our Products: Micro Tiller

Maintenance is the cheapest way to keep a machine working

A micro tiller is a simple machine: engine, gearbox, blades, handle. Almost every failure that stops one in the field comes from one of four things — dirty air filter, loose hardware, worn drive parts, or a blade that has bent into the soil. None of those need a workshop, and all of them can be prevented by an hour of attention spread through the season.

Below is the schedule we work to, and it applies to every machine in our range: the 170cc petrol 1WG-4.0AQ, the 178cc diesel 1WG-4.1A and the 186cc diesel 1WG-6.3A all share the same construction, so the routine is identical.

Before every working day: five minutes

  • Fuel and oil. Check that there is enough fuel in the 4.5 L tank and that the engine oil is at the correct level. A machine that starts and runs for ten minutes with an empty tank wastes more time than the check costs.
  • Air filter. Check that the intake cover is clean. A blocked filter is the most common reason an engine runs rough and burns fuel badly, and it is also the cheapest thing to fix.
  • Fuel tap and lines. Make sure the tap is open and that no water or dirt has collected in the filter bowl. Water in the fuel is what turns a machine that started yesterday into a machine that will not start today.
  • Blades and tines. Look along the blade set for bent or missing tines, and remove any stones or wire wrapped around the rotor. This is the check that prevents the expensive damage.
  • Guards and fasteners. Check that the chain or belt cover is in place and that the handle bolts and depth-adjustment pins are tight. A loose fastener on a vibrating handle never gets tighter.

Weekly: an hour with a spanner

  • Check the drive. The friction-plate clutch, the belt or chain, and the gearbox input. Look for glazing, cracking or a loose adjustment. Replace a belt while it still drives properly, rather than when it snaps in the middle of a row.
  • Grease the moving joints. The depth-adjustment linkage, the handle pivot and the wheel shaft. This is what stops the handle from seizing in a wet season and what keeps the depth setting repeatable from one pass to the next.
  • Inspect the blades. Replace tines that have worn below the point of usefulness, and balance the set if a group is noticeably worn on one side. Unbalanced blades cause vibration that loosens everything else on the machine.
  • Check the gearbox oil level and the condition of the oil. The gearbox is the part that carries the daily work, and it is a sealed unit on every machine in the range — it is worth looking at before it becomes a replacement.
  • Check the fuel filter and clean it if the fuel system has been running on dirty fuel.

Monthly and at the end of the season

  • Air cleaner. Remove and clean the filter element rather than just blowing on it. A working filter is the difference between an engine that starts on the first pull and one that needs three.
  • Spark plug. Remove, check and clean the plug, and check the gap against the manual supplied with the machine. A fouled plug is the classic cause of hard starting and poor throttle response.
  • Carburettor and fuel lines. If the engine has been running poorly, clean the air filter first and the carburettor second. On a diesel, check that the pre-cleaner on the fuel supply is clear.
  • Check the gearbox oil again and look for any metal in it. Fresh oil with metal in it means something inside has been wearing, and it is worth finding out before it becomes a failure.
  • Confirm the clutch and the reverse gear. Both should engage cleanly. A gear that hesitates is usually a linkage adjustment rather than a worn part.
  • Off-season storage. Drain the fuel if the machine will stand for weeks, clean the machine, oil the exposed metal, cover the intake, and store it dry and off the ground. A machine stored damp will show rust from the first month.

The spare parts to keep on the shelf

For any operator running a machine daily, the list is short and it never changes: spare blades and tines, a drive belt or chain set, a set of standard bolts and nuts, a spark plug, an air filter element, a fuel filter, and a good quality fuel line. Keep these in the toolbox, not in a warehouse, because a machine that stops in a field is only fixed by what is in the vehicle with it.

A note on warranty and service

Every machine in the range carries a 1 year warranty. Because the tiller, the ditching machines and the water pumps share a common design, wear parts and consumables are consistent across models, which makes stocking spares simpler for a dealer and simpler for the operator.

The practical rule that matters most: grease it, check the air filter, and keep the blades sharp. Machines that are looked after start when the operator expects them to start, and they are still working years later.

Frequently asked questions

Answers to the questions buyers ask us most.

How often should a micro tiller be serviced?

Three intervals. Five minutes before every working day to check fuel, oil, the air filter, the fuel tap and lines, the blades and the guards. About an hour weekly on the drive, the greased joints, the blades, the gearbox oil and the fuel filter. Monthly and at the end of the season for the air cleaner, the spark plug, the carburettor, the gearbox oil and off-season storage.

What is the most common cause of a tiller that will not start?

The fuel system. Check the tank, that the tap is open, that no water or dirt has collected in the filter bowl, then the air filter, the choke and the spark plug. Water in the fuel is what turns a machine that started yesterday into one that will not start today.

Why does the engine run rough and burn fuel badly?

A blocked air filter. It is the most common reason for it and also the cheapest thing to fix. Remove and clean the filter element rather than just blowing on it.

How often should I check the gearbox oil?

Weekly and again monthly. Look for metal in it as well as the level. Fresh oil with metal in it means something inside has been wearing, and it is worth finding out before it becomes a failure.

Which spare parts should I keep in the toolbox?

Spare blades and tines, a drive belt or chain set, a set of standard bolts and nuts, a spark plug, an air filter element, a fuel filter, and a good quality fuel line. Keep them in the vehicle, not in a warehouse, because a machine that stops in a field is only fixed by what is in the vehicle with it.

How do I store the machine over the dry season?

Drain the fuel if the machine will stand for weeks, clean the machine, oil the exposed metal, cover the intake, and store it dry and off the ground. A machine stored damp will show rust from the first month.

What causes vibration in a micro tiller?

Almost always a bent tine or a worn blade group. Check the blades before you look anywhere else, and balance the set if a group is noticeably worn on one side.

Do all your machines share the same maintenance routine?

Yes. The 170cc petrol 1WG-4.0AQ, the 178cc diesel 1WG-4.1A and the 186cc diesel 1WG-6.3A all share the same construction, and because the tillers, the ditching machines and the water pumps share a common design, wear parts and consumables are consistent across models. Every machine carries a 1 year warranty.
